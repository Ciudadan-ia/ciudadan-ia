## A machine for continuing sentences

Imagine someone who has read almost everything on the internet: books, newspapers, forums,
recipes, laws. They didn't memorize every text, but they did learn the patterns: after
"once upon a" almost always comes "time"; after a medical question usually comes an answer
with a certain tone. A large language model (LLM) is that: a mathematical system trained to
predict the next word.

When you type a question, it doesn't look up the answer in a database. It *generates* it,
word by word, each time choosing the most likely continuation given everything it has
read. That the result sounds intelligent is not magic: human language has an enormous
amount of structure, and the machine learned it.

## Why it gets so much right

Scale changes things. With enough data and enough computing power, "predicting the next
word" produces abilities that surprised even the researchers: translating, summarizing,
writing code, explaining a topic in simple words. That is why these tools feel genuinely
useful: for drafting a letter, understanding a contract or preparing a class, they save
hours.

## Why it fails so badly

The same mechanism explains its failures. The model does not distinguish between what it
knows and what it doesn't: ask it about a law that doesn't exist, and it can "cite" it,
article number and all, because its task is to continue the text plausibly. This is called
*hallucination*, and it is not a rare defect: it is part of the design. It also inherits
the biases of the texts it was trained on — and most of those texts are in English and
come from a small part of the world.

## What this means for you

Three practical rules. First, use it as a draft, never as a final source — verify facts,
dates, laws and medicines against real sources. Second, don't hand it private information
about yourself or others: what you type may be used to train future models. Third,
remember who is answering: not a neutral sage, but a statistical mirror of the internet's
texts, with its accuracies and its prejudices.

AI that writes is neither an oracle nor a fraud: it is a new tool, powerful and
sharp-edged. Knowing the mechanism — predicting the next word — is the best protection for
using it without being used.
