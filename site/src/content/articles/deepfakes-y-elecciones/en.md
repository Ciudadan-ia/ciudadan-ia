## What exactly is a deepfake

A deepfake is a video, audio clip or image fabricated with AI that shows a real person
doing or saying something that never happened. The technology learns from authentic
recordings — a few minutes of voice or a few dozen photos are enough — and then generates
new content with that face and that voice. What required a Hollywood studio five years ago
is now done with an app.

## Why it matters in an election

Election season is perfect terrain: emotions run high, there is little time to verify, and
many people are sharing. A fake audio of a candidate "admitting" fraud, released the night
before the vote, can travel through WhatsApp to millions of phones before any rebuttal
exists. Several countries in the region have already documented such cases — cloned audio,
manipulated video, fake news bulletins read by synthetic voices.

There is a second, quieter harm: once people know deepfakes exist, any politician can
dismiss a real, inconvenient video by saying "that's AI." The lie wins twice: when the
fake is believed, and when the truth is doubted.

## What no longer works

For a while, tricks were taught: watch the blinking, the hands, the edges of the face.
They are no longer reliable — the models improved. Automatic detectors are no guarantee
either: they succeed sometimes, fail other times, and always arrive late.

## What does work

The defense is habits, not technology. First: look at the **channel**. Was it published by
an identifiable outlet, or did it arrive forwarded with no origin? Content that exists
only in messaging chains deserves maximum suspicion. Second: find the **original source**
— if the candidate really said it, a serious outlet will have it. Third: **wait**.
Manipulation bets on your immediate reaction; sharing an hour later, once verified, takes
no force away from the truth, but it does from the lie. And fourth: when in doubt, don't
share — not spreading something false is also participation.
